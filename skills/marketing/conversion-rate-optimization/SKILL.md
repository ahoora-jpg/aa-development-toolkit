# Conversion Rate Optimization (CRO)

Status: Draft
Last reviewed: 2026-07-26

## Purpose

How to turn more of a site's existing visitors into actual customers, rather than only trying to attract more traffic. For most e-commerce stores this is the highest-leverage lever available: improving the percentage of visitors who buy is usually cheaper than acquiring more visitors through ads or SEO.

## Core Concepts To Apply

- **Know the baseline before optimizing**: the average e-commerce conversion rate sits around 2–3% globally, and average cart abandonment runs roughly 70% across studied sites — treat these as a baseline for comparison, not a target, and measure your own site's actual numbers before assuming what's broken.
- **A strong product page needs four elements**: multiple high-quality images (several angles plus close-ups, not just one hero shot), benefit-driven descriptions (not just a spec sheet — see `skills/marketing/content-copywriting/`), visible social proof (reviews, ratings), and clear urgency/availability signals (stock level, delivery estimate) — pages missing several of these convert measurably worse.
- **Checkout friction is the single biggest fixable leak**: forcing account creation before checkout, hidden shipping costs revealed only at the final step, and long/unclear multi-page checkouts are the most common causes of abandoned carts — offering guest checkout and showing total cost early are two of the highest-impact fixes available.
- **Mobile experience determines most of the outcome**: mobile makes up the majority of e-commerce traffic and a large share of purchases — treat mobile checkout and product-page usability as the primary experience to test, not a secondary check after desktop (connects directly to `skills/ui-ux/mobile-first-responsive/`).
- **Test changes, don't guess**: CRO is a repeatable cycle — analyze real user behavior (heatmaps, session recordings, analytics), form a specific hypothesis ("adding a delivery estimate will increase add-to-cart rate because unclear shipping is a common abandonment reason"), test one change at a time, then implement what actually moves the metric.
- **Trust signals reduce hesitation**: reviews, clear warranty/return policy, and recognizable payment/security badges matter especially for a first-time buyer on an unfamiliar store — for a brand built on "50 years of family trust," make that trust story visible at the exact moment of purchase decision, not buried on an About page.

## When To Use

Apply the four product-page elements and checkout-friction fixes from initial launch — these are well-established baseline practices, not experimental ideas. Move to structured A/B testing once the site has enough traffic to reach statistical significance (testing without enough visitors just produces noise, not real answers).
